## Opponent strategies

This section is a collection of ideas for later strategy work. Do not change the current algorithm solely because these notes exist.

### + Beginning — current behavior

John observed this example: Wendy holds one Snickers and the human holds two. Wendy requests Snickers, receives one, and therefore now holds two while the human holds one. On her continued turn, she sometimes asks for another type, misses, and loses the turn. The human can infer that Wendy still holds Snickers, request it twice, and claim the set. Classify this current behavior as **Beginning (+)**. The current weighted strategy can favor a pair but does not guarantee the follow-up request; its memory also clears known human possession after a one-candy transfer.

### ++ / +++ — stronger follow-up inference

When Wendy receives a candy without completing its set, she should consider requesting that type again immediately, especially when she now has two. In John's example, repeating Snickers would collect the set instead of exposing her pair to the human. From Wendy's restricted information, receiving one alone does not reveal the human's original count: the third copy might still be with the human or in the dish. Public evidence and previous requests may narrow that uncertainty. A repeat request can be a strong choice without a universal guarantee, because it pursues an immediate set and avoids switching away from a newly exposed pair.

John wants the improvement classified above Beginning, at **Medium (++) or Advanced (+++)**. Exact separation between those levels is still to be designed. Ideas include retaining better evidence after one-candy transfers, valuing immediate set completion, and considering the risk of handing a known pair back to the human by asking for something else and missing. Never grant a stronger opponent access to the human hand or dish order.

### Memory strength ideas

John proposes configurable memory of the human's candies: **Beginning (+): 0%; Medium (++): 50%; Advanced (+++): 100%**. These are proposed values for later implementation, not descriptions of the existing algorithm (which already retains some public-event memory).

Distinguish strong direct evidence from other clues. If the human receives a candy from the opponent, the opponent knows the human has that type unless the receipt completes and removes its set. John suggests ++/+++ may always retain this direct transfer evidence, while weaker clues can be remembered or forgotten probabilistically. A human request also proves possession at that moment. An opponent request receiving just one candy does not establish how many copies the human still holds afterward.

Before implementation, decide whether the percentage applies uniformly to all evidence or whether direct transfers are a guaranteed-memory exception for ++/+++. Also define whether 50% means a chance to store a clue when observed or a chance to retain it as later moves occur. Avoid rolling memory independently on every decision without a clear reason; that can cause a forgotten fact to mysteriously reappear. Use explicit stored observations with controlled forgetting, so the behavior remains understandable and testable.

Even 100% memory means perfect recall of public observations, not knowledge of hidden draws or the current human hand. Invalidate or revise evidence after relevant transfers and completed sets. Unknown human draws weaken absence evidence. Stronger levels can combine better recall with the follow-up strategy above. No memory-level settings have been implemented.

### Future opponent-level selector

John proposes arrows near the opponent that cycle among three strategy levels, displayed as **+**, **++**, and **+++**. This is an in-game UI idea, not a change to the shell's binary switches. Decide later whether levels are chosen before a deal or can change mid-round. Keep stable internal roles independent of opponent names/portraits. No selector or algorithm upgrade is implemented yet.
